Choosing a Microphone for Streaming, Podcasts and Calls

Last updated 8 August 2026 — MicTest.live

Microphone recommendations tend to be lists of products. The more useful thing to understand is which characteristics matter for your situation, because the right microphone for an untreated bedroom is different from the right microphone for a quiet office, and both differ from what a studio needs.

Start with your room, not the microphone

This is the decision that determines everything else. A sensitive microphone in a reflective room records the room. A less sensitive, more directional microphone in the same room records mostly you.

Be honest about your space. Do you have bare walls, a hard floor, a large window? Is there a computer fan running, traffic outside, other people in the house? If the answer to several of those is yes, you want a dynamic microphone used close up. If you have a quiet, soft-furnished room, a condenser will give you more detail and a more open sound.

People routinely buy a well-reviewed condenser, hear their room in every recording, and conclude the microphone is bad. The microphone is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Condenser versus dynamic

Polar patterns in one paragraph

The polar pattern describes which directions a microphone listens to. Cardioid picks up from the front and rejects the rear — this is what you want for almost every solo application. Omnidirectional picks up equally from everywhere, useful for a group around a table and disastrous in a noisy room. Bidirectional picks up front and rear, for two people facing each other. Shotgun is highly directional, used at a distance on camera.

If your microphone has a pattern switch, leaving it on omnidirectional when you meant cardioid is one of the most common self-inflicted noise problems. Check it.

What to spend, honestly

The relationship between price and result is steeply diminishing. From $0 to about $100 the improvement is dramatic — a $90 microphone crushes a laptop's built-in array microphone. From $100 to $300 the improvement is real but much smaller. Beyond $300, differences are mostly about character and specific use cases rather than better sound in a home setting.

Meanwhile the same money spent elsewhere often does more:

  • A boom arm. Lets you position the microphone close to your mouth without hunching over the desk, and isolates it from desk vibration. Improves every recording from the moment it is installed.
  • A pop filter. Removes plosive thumps that no software fixes cleanly. Costs almost nothing.
  • Something soft behind you. A curtain, a bookcase, a duvet on a stand. Reduces the reflections that make home recordings sound amateurish.
  • Headphones. Eliminate echo on calls entirely, and let you actually hear what you are recording.

A $90 microphone on a boom arm with a pop filter in a soft corner beats a $400 microphone sitting on a bare desk in a bare room. This is not close.

Matching the microphone to what you do

Video calls and meetings. A wired headset with a boom is genuinely hard to beat: consistent distance, close to the mouth, and headphones built in so echo is impossible. Unglamorous and extremely effective.

Streaming. A USB dynamic microphone on a boom arm. You need rejection of keyboard and fan noise more than you need detail, and you need it to work every time without an interface to configure.

Podcasting with guests. This is where XLR earns its place, because you need several microphones into one device. A multi-input interface plus dynamic microphones is the standard answer for a reason.

Music and voiceover. A condenser in the best-treated space you have, with the processing switched off and gain set manually.

Before you buy anything

Run the microphone test with what you already own. Check the noise floor while silent, record five seconds, and listen with headphones. Two things often come out of this: the existing microphone is better than you thought and the real problem is distance or room, or the noise floor is so high that no microphone would help until the room is addressed.

Either way you will know what you are actually buying to fix, which is a much better position than choosing from a list.

Polar patterns, and why the cheap ones matter

A polar pattern describes which directions a microphone listens to, and choosing the right one does more for your sound than most price increases.

Cardioid picks up mostly from the front and rejects the rear. It is the correct choice for essentially all solo speech, and if a microphone offers only one pattern this should be it.

Omnidirectional hears equally in all directions. It captures the room, which is exactly what you do not want unless the room is treated, and it is why the built-in laptop microphone sounds hollow.

Bidirectional hears front and back — useful for two people facing each other across a table, useless otherwise.

Multi-pattern microphones advertise this as a feature, and for one person in an untreated room it is a liability: the pattern switch is one more thing to knock into the wrong position. A single well-implemented cardioid beats a switchable pattern that spends its life set incorrectly.

The accessories that outperform an upgrade

Once past a basic competent microphone, the ranked list of things that improve how you sound looks nothing like a list of microphones.

A boom arm comes first, because it lets you position the microphone 15 to 20 cm from your mouth and slightly off-axis without hunching toward a desk stand. Every problem caused by distance disappears at that range.

A pop filter or foam windscreen comes second. Plosives — the burst of air on P and B sounds — are the single most common recording flaw, and they cannot be fixed afterwards. A filter costs almost nothing and eliminates them.

Something soft behind you comes third. A curtain, a bookshelf, an acoustic panel, even a duvet during a recording. Hard parallel walls create the reflections that make a voice sound like it was recorded in a bathroom, and the wall you face is the one that matters most.

Closed-back headphones come fourth, and they are non-negotiable if anyone else is speaking. They make echo physically impossible and let you hear problems while they are still fixable.

What each budget realistically buys

Under $60. A basic USB dynamic or a decent headset. Placement will matter more than the model — sit close and you will sound fine on calls.

$60–$150. The sweet spot for most people. A well-regarded USB dynamic plus a boom arm, and the result is good enough for published podcasts.

$150–$400. Better build quality, a broadcast-style dynamic, or an entry XLR interface with a good microphone. Real but modest gains over the previous tier.

Above $400. Only worth it once the room is treated and the technique is solid, because past this point the room is the limiting factor and no microphone fixes a room.
